Building OpenFLUME with AI

November 2026

I don't care where a good idea comes from, and for years I let that stretch into not caring how anything was made, only how good it was. Yet one spot of chatbot phrasing and I throw the whole message out, and some respect for the sender with it. So quality can't be all that matters.

Unless quality is more than one thing.

Some writing is functional. A table of fluid properties, a proof, a diff. Its value is in what it says, and nobody cares who typed it. On a pull request every edit serves the thing being built, and AI is one more tool in the pile. The only fair question is whether the result is good.

Other writing is personal. Its value is that a particular person thought it, felt it, and is telling you. Feedback from someone who knows your work lands differently than the same advice in a book. Ask them what they think and get back something pasted from a chatbot, and it is a betrayal. You asked for connection and they shooed you away like a pest.

The more personal the exchange, the more AI in it cheapens it.

That puts OpenFLUME at one end and this post at the other. A thermo-fluid solver is about as functional as a thing gets. Nobody running a tank blowdown cares whether I or a model wrote the backward-Euler step. They care whether the pressure history is right. If you're reading this, you want to know what I think about building an engineering tool this way.

Before launch, an AI agent drafted the docs, the blog post, and a preprint from what I dictated, and the drafts came back full of lines like "Convergence is reported rather than assumed" and "Here is my honest one-paragraph version." Those are the model's notes to itself about staying on task, and it left them in as prose with my own phrasing sitting right there. It cut them once I could describe what was wrong, but most people won't push back that far because it's easier not to. I rewrote this post until it said what I think.2

It isn't that I use AI for writing because I don't care. I use it because I do. What I write by hand rarely meets my own standard, and sometimes that stops me from putting anything down at all. A model gets something on the canvas. It also gives me permission. When neither of us can come up with something better, I can accept that a sentence is good enough.

The solver was the easy call. I had wanted an open source one like this since I wrote a basic one at Virgin Galactic more than ten years ago. I knew roughly how to build and test it, and every few years I chipped away at a draft, but it was more work than one person could get far on. With AI, I built it in about ten weeks.1

I couldn't have done it as well without AI. I would have let AI do it all, if it could. It couldn't.

Where AI mattered most was the evidence that the solver is right. Every closure needs a reference case, and someone has to dig that reference out of a NASA report or a paper, set it up, and decide what agreement means. OpenFLUME has that across six disciplines, from incompressible hydraulics to combustion, including cryogenic chilldown checked against measured traces from NBS Report 9264. Alone I would have covered a fraction of it.

What the models needed from me was judgment. LLMs are good at adding and bad at subtracting, and subtracting is most of what good engineering is. Which terms belong in the energy balance, which closure is right, when the simpler formulation is the correct one, what counts as agreement: those calls were mine.

They also struggled to see that a result was wrong. A plot can be smooth and plausible with a wrong sign in it, and a model is not yet as good as a person at noticing.

Many of the worst defects in the CHANGELOG were the same defect: something reported success when it hadn't succeeded. The latest was a Fanno duct that reported "Converged" on a nonphysical answer, with Mach jumping from 0.70 to 1.40 halfway down a constant-area pipe while the second-law audit stayed silent. The fix ships in the next release. Several of the fixes have had the same shape, a silent fallback replaced with a loud failure.

The record has limits. Most of it is analytic verification, and the experimental side is narrow. On saturated liquid-nitrogen chilldown, OpenFLUME's mean absolute error is 12.8 % against 8.8 % for GFSSP's published predictions. GFSSP is better there (§8.4). If you find a case where OpenFLUME disagrees with a reference you trust, open an issue with the reference and the tolerance.

What AI could not do was decide what to build, take things out, or notice when an answer was wrong. Those were mine, and so is this post.
